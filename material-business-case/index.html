Internal research memorandum · Goodera

Material

An assessment of the market for corporate donation kits, Goodera's existing position within it, and the case for constituting kit supply as an independent business unit.

Prepared for Abhishek Humbad
Pratik Sagar Mishra · August 2026
Scope: United States primary, India secondary

This memorandum tests a single proposition: that the physical kit consumed inside a Goodera volunteering event is a business in its own right, and is currently being operated as a cost line. It is written to be argued with. Figures are sourced throughout; where a figure could not be verified against a primary source, it is marked as such rather than smoothed over.

1Summary of findings

Seven findings, in descending order of how much they should change the decision.

The central conclusion

The question is not whether Goodera could build a kit business. Finding 1 establishes that it already runs one, at gross margins between 40% and 60% after shipping — above every promotional-products and contract-logistics benchmark available, and inside the range occupied by branded consumer toy manufacturers.

The question is whether that operation is given a catalogue, a price list, a balance sheet and an owner — or continues to be invoiced as cost of delivery.

2The donation kit market

2.1 There is no single market figure, and it is worth saying so plainly

No research house publishes a "donation kit market" estimate. Any figure claiming to size this category directly should be treated as fabricated. What exists instead are four adjacent, independently measured pools, all of which pay for the same physical object. Triangulating between them is the honest method.

The relevant observation is not that any one pool is large. It is that the market Goodera currently addresses is the smallest line in the table, and that the kit is the only asset in the business capable of reaching the others.

2.2 The best single proxy for physical-goods flow

Good360, the largest intermediary of corporate product donation in the United States, distributed $4.0 billion in goods in 2024, against $3.0 billion in 2023 and $2.5 billion in 2022 — growth of 20% then 33%. This is the closest available measure of how much physical product corporations are moving into charitable channels, and it is accelerating.

Read this figure correctly

Good360's $4.0B is the assessed value of donated goods, not revenue. Its IRS Form 990 for FY2024 shows total revenue of $3.26 billion of which 99.2% is in-kind valuation; actual cash program-service revenue is $24.7 million. The goods move at enormous scale. Almost nobody is charging for moving them. §4 returns to why this matters.

2.3 Demand is moving toward the format a kit fits

Three independent movements in corporate volunteering all favour a bounded, hands-on, shippable activity.

Volunteering is becoming shorter, more frequent, more physical and more self-directed. A kit build is the only common format that satisfies all four conditions simultaneously: it takes 60–90 minutes, it produces a countable object, it happens in a room, and it can be completed without a facilitator present.

2.4 The formats in market

The kit is a container format. The cause varies; the operation — source, assemble, pack, ship, distribute, report — does not. Goodera already operates four of the ten largest formats.

3Where the money comes from

Six distinct corporate budgets can fund the same kit. Only one of them is the CSR budget Goodera sells into today. This matters more than market size, because it determines how many separate approval paths a kit product can travel.

3.1 The tax mechanic underneath the market

Under 26 U.S.C. §170(e)(3) — verified against the statute — a C corporation donating inventory to a 501(c)(3), where the goods are used solely for the care of the ill, the needy or infants, may deduct its cost basis plus one half of the appreciation, capped at twice basis.

The consequence is that for inventory a company would otherwise liquidate at a heavy discount, donation is frequently the financially superior disposal route. This is the structural reason product-donation intermediaries exist at multi-billion-dollar scale.

Why this is strategically unusual

The wider the spread between a kit's retail price and its cost, the larger the buyer's deduction — up to the cap. Supplier margin and buyer tax benefit move in the same direction. This is the rare case where pricing higher makes the product cheaper for the customer.

4Competitive structure

Thirty organisations were profiled, using IRS Form 990 filings for nonprofits and published pricing and third-party revenue estimates for private companies. The category divides cleanly along two axes: whether a player owns its corporate demand, and whether it designs its own product.

4.1 Two observations that follow

  • The demand exists and is not being charged for. The $0 program-service-revenue pattern across nonprofit kit engines is a genuine reporting artefact, not missing data — event fees are classified as contributions. Corporates are paying for kit builds at very large volume; the recipients are not booking it as commercial revenue.
  • Scale in this category is not a barrier Goodera needs to overcome. At approximately $35M revenue, Goodera is already larger than the entire commercial field. The competitive question is not whether Goodera can reach the scale of incumbents; it is why incumbents at a fraction of the size have catalogues and published prices, and Goodera does not.

5Goodera's existing position

5.1 The capability is already built and already paid for

Every structural barrier to entering a physical kit business is a capability Goodera currently operates. This is drawn from Goodera's own published material, not from internal claims.

Goodera's own marketing already sells "kit procurement and shipping services" and "pre-event logistics management" as lines of business, supported by what it describes as a global logistics and fulfilment network. The sourcing relationships, the freight and customs capability, and the distribution network are in place and in use.

Restating the proposal precisely

This is not a proposal to enter a supply chain business. Goodera is already running one, in 100+ countries, at 40–60% gross margin, and reporting it as cost of delivery.

5.2 One kit generates multiple events

The commercial behaviour of a kit inside Goodera's existing model is already documented. A single "Assemble a Solar Lamp" activity, repackaged as a goal-based campaign — "A Thousand Suns" — became 1,000 kits across five events for one customer. The robotics line reached 989 units across 70 cities, 4,945 students and 66 nonprofit partners.

The mechanism is worth naming: a kit gives a campaign a unit of account. A scoreboard requires something countable. Every packaging success Goodera has recorded has been built on top of a physical unit.

6Margin analysis

This section rests on figures provided internally in August 2026: median gross margin across kits of 40%, rising to 60% for kits Goodera designs itself, both stated after shipping costs. These are the most consequential numbers in the memorandum and they change the argument materially.

6.1 Where 40–60% sits against comparable businesses

Two conclusions follow directly.

  • Goodera's kit operation does not resemble a logistics business. It resembles a branded consumer products business. At 60% on own-design kits it sits between Mattel and Hasbro, and roughly four times the margin of commoditised contract kitting.
  • The 20-point gap between bought-in and own-design kits is the strategy, already proven. The argument for owning design rather than reselling does not need to be made from theory or from external comparables. It has been measured inside Goodera's own book.

7The proposed business

Material is proposed as a business unit with its own catalogue, bill of materials, margin and owner. It sells into Goodera events as it does today, and additionally into channels that require no event.

7.1 Launch narrative

The following is written as a future press release, as a test of whether the proposition is coherent and worth building. It is a design device, not a communications draft.

Illustrative · September 2027

Goodera launches Material, and puts a price on the box.

The company that has shipped impact kits into 100+ countries for a decade begins selling them as a product — with a catalogue, published prices, and four ways to buy that do not require booking an event.

For ten years the most memorable part of a Goodera volunteering event has been the object people made: a solar lamp, a robot, a relief kit, a plush toy a child taught to speak. That object travelled further than the event did — into a photograph, a CSR report, a classroom. And for ten years it was invoiced as a cost.

Material designs its own kits, owns its bill of materials, manufactures at volume out of India and holds stock in region. Customers can book a Material kit as a volunteering event exactly as before, or now: order from a catalogue and run the build themselves; replace a swag line with a kit that gets donated rather than discarded; fund a standing disaster reserve that ships the day a storm makes landfall; or buy the give-side units behind their own one-for-one commitment.

"We have been running a supply chain and calling it an events business. The kit was never the cost of the experience. It was the part people kept."

Material launches with six kit lines and a published price list — the first in a category where nearly every competitor quotes on request.

"We had three line items: a volunteering vendor, a swag vendor, and a disaster-relief donation. Material collapsed them into one purchase order." — launch customer, Fortune 100 Head of Social Impact

The narrative commits the business to four testable claims: a published price list in a quote-only category; an owned bill of materials manufactured at volume; in-region stock; and buying motions that do not depend on an event. Each is addressed below.

8Revenue models

Six models, each drawing on a different budget, each running on the same bill of materials. Ordered by expected ease of execution.

01

The catalogue

Ship the kit against a published price. No host, no booking, no event. This strips out the only genuinely expensive input — a trained person in the room — and is the highest-margin form of the product.

Goodera would be the only entrant able to launch a catalogue with 500+ Fortune customers already holding an account. Every competitor's hardest problem is demand; Goodera's is a SKU number.

Proof: Odyssey Teams DIY kits $275–$1,925 · Impact 4 Good, 19 published SKUs from $24 to $2,880, plus a $250 "find me a charity" add-on · Project Helping Kynd Kit $25. Goodera currently publishes no prices.
02

The swag substitution

The US promotional products market is $26.6B, owned by HR, marketing and events rather than CSR. Its sustainable segment is $3.69B and growing 20% a year — the fastest-moving line in the industry. Between 69% and 75% of employees state a preference for sustainably made swag.

A kit is a swag substitute that is built, photographed and donated. Same budget line, no new approval. This is the fastest available route to first revenue.

Proof: Packed with Purpose charges $3–5 to brand a box, $5–7 with a card — branding is priced as pure margin on an object already purchased.
03

The standing reserve

Disaster relief is not purchased during a disaster. The American Red Cross operates an Annual Disaster Giving Program in which corporates pre-commit fixed annual sums that stock warehouses ahead of the season; Mattress Firm pledges $250,000 a year, and Amazon's tarp donation covered the Red Cross's entire forecast annual need.

For a supplier this is the most favourable revenue shape available: a recurring purchase order against inventory manufactured on a planned rather than reactive schedule. Goodera already runs disaster relief as an activity category.

Proof: Good360 moved $5.75M in relief goods within weeks of Hurricanes Helene and Milton, funded by Amazon, United, UPS, PwC, CVS, IKEA and Lowe's · Direct Relief holds $926M in standing inventory against $2.4B annual deployment.
04

The give side

Buy-one-give-one brands sell the consumer half and must then source, assemble and distribute the donated half — a supply chain problem they rarely wish to own. Warby Parker is the clearest documented case: it funds VisionSpring to produce and distribute the given pair rather than manufacturing it.

This is the one model where Goodera's nonprofit network is not a distribution channel but the product itself: verified last-mile delivery across 100+ countries with impact reporting attached.

Proof: Bombas 150M+ items donated, 4,000 partners · Warby Parker 20M+ pairs · Cuddle+Kind, 10 meals per doll, the closest structural match in market.
05

Institutional procurement

Agencies and governments buy kits by the hundred thousand on published tenders, with no volunteering event involved. UNICEF's School-in-a-Box is a 42-item kit for 40 students and a teacher; over 100,000 were delivered in 2024, reaching 5 million children.

A business with an owned bill of materials and Indian manufacturing is a natural supplier here. The barrier is registration and quality certification, not capability or demand.

Proof: UNICEF Supply Division procurement $5.61B in 2024, +7% YoY; UNGM vendor registration is free · India: Atal Tinkering Labs, 10,000 labs at ₹12 lakh each · GeM cumulative ₹18.4 lakh crore.
06

The tax vehicle

Material can sit on both sides of the §170(e)(3) trade. Inbound, corporate overstock taken as kit components arrives at a tax-subsidised effective cost. Outbound, the kit is packaged so the deduction is defensible in a single pass by the buyer's tax function.

Good360 built a $3.26B flow on precisely this mechanic while booking only $24.7M in cash revenue — it productised the paperwork and gave away the product. The compliance layer is billable in its own right.

Proof: 26 U.S.C. §170(e)(3) · Good360 IRS Form 990 FY2024 · NAEIR, $4B+ facilitated across 60,000 member nonprofits.

The argument is not that any individual channel is large. It is that all six run on one bill of materials, one factory relationship, one warehouse network and one customer list — and none requires a volunteering booking to occur first.

9Risks and constraints

Four material risks. Each specifies a design decision rather than defeating the proposal.

Summarised: own the brand, stock in region, contract per unit, sell the proof with the product.

10India

10.1 Demand

India is the only major economy in which corporate social spending is legally mandated, which makes the demand recurring and forecastable rather than discretionary.

10.2 The solar lamp programme, stated accurately

In July 2016 the Government announced assistance of ₹1,800 crore to scale IIT Bombay's Solar Urja Lamp model to 100 million households. That figure is frequently cited as a programme value. It should not be.

  • The ₹1,800 crore was an announced scale-up commitment. No PIB, MNRE or IIT Bombay document confirming formal sanction or disbursement at that scale could be located.
  • The 100-million target was not reached. Verified execution across the family of schemes totals approximately 2.0–2.7 million lamps.
  • What was executed is well documented. The 70 Lakh Solar Study Lamp Scheme: ₹494 crore sanctioned, 61.01 lakh lamps distributed across five states, at approximately ₹450 per lamp subsidised to ₹100. The preceding "Million SoUL" phase distributed 1 million lamps through 54 assembly centres and 350 repair centres.
The relevant precedent is operational, not financial

Approximately 1,300 women were employed assembling these lamps in village-level centres, trained to assemble, distribute and repair. India has already demonstrated distributed kit assembly at seven-figure unit volumes. That is a more useful proof point for Material than the headline figure, and it survives scrutiny.

10.3 A legal constraint that changes the pitch

In-kind donation does not qualify as CSR expenditure in India

Under MCA General Circular 01/2016, Q.15, a contribution in kind cannot be monetised and counted as CSR spend. This follows from the wording of Section 135(5) of the Companies Act 2013 itself — "shall ensure that the company spends" — which is why no subsequent amendment to the Rules has altered it.

The India proposition is therefore never "donate our kits." It is cash CSR project expenditure with kits as the deliverable, routed through a CSR-1-registered implementing partner and reported under Rule 8. Administrative overhead is separately capped at 5% of CSR expenditure under Rule 7(1).

10.4 Supply

Goodera's re-domiciliation to India was undertaken for listing reasons. A kit business makes it an operating advantage as well.

No US competitor can match this position. Odyssey Teams, TeamBonding and Impact 4 Good all purchase finished goods at Western wholesale prices. Material would be the only operator in the category with its cost base inside an active manufacturing subsidy regime.

There is also a narrative consequence. For an Indian listing, "a US corporate software company domiciled in India" is a difficult story. "India designs and manufactures the world's social-impact kits and sells them to the Fortune 500" describes a category Indian public markets have repeatedly funded: product, intellectual property, exports and a domestic manufacturing base. If Material is principal on kit sales, revenue is recognised gross, which raises headline scale.

11Decisions required

11.1 Outstanding information

Two questions remain open and would sharpen, though not alter, the analysis.

  • What proportion of current revenue is kit-bearing? This determines whether Material is a new venture or a restatement of a substantial existing business. At 40–60% margins the distinction is commercially significant.
  • Are kits currently bought finished, or assembled by Goodera? The 40%/60% split implies both occur. The ratio between them sets the size of the immediate margin opportunity from converting bought-in lines to own-design.
Closing observation

Goodera is the only organisation positioned to build this, and the reason is not manufacturing. It is that demand is the hard part of a kit business, and Goodera already owns it: 500+ Fortune customers, 50,000 nonprofit partners, 100+ countries, and a decade of shipping the box.

Every other operator in this category has to find the customer. Goodera has to find the SKU.

ASources and confidence

A.1 Method

Five parallel research streams: US market sizing; corporate giving mechanics; competitive landscape via IRS Form 990 filings; India market and regulation; and revenue-model precedents. Approximately 30 organisations profiled. Nonprofit financials are drawn from Form 990 filings via ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer for FY2024–FY2025.

A.2 Figures carrying reduced confidence

  • ₹1,800 crore solar lamp figure — an announced 2016 commitment, not a verified disbursement. Presented as such in §10.2.
  • For-profit competitor revenues — third-party estimates (Growjo, ZoomInfo, Owler) which disagree by 2–5×. Stated as ranges.
  • "$4–7 billion in unclaimed matching gifts" — traces to a single software vendor with no published methodology. Not relied on in this memorandum.
  • Cause-marketing spend — the most recent verifiable primary figure dates to 2019. Excluded from Table 2 for that reason.
  • Current CECP non-cash share of corporate giving — the most recent published series runs 2007–2012. Not used.
  • ATL expansion to 50,000 labs — widely reported following Budget 2025-26 but not confirmable on any primary government source. Excluded from §10.1.
  • Goodera margin figures — internal, provided August 2026, stated after shipping. Not independently verifiable and load-bearing for §6. Worth confirming against management accounts before external use.
